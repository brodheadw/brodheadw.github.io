What you're looking at

A 3D map of the nearby universe built from public survey data. It asks one question: do the directions of high-energy neutrinos and gamma rays line up with nearby galaxies? Right now it focuses on Boötes III, a dwarf galaxy 46 kpc away that is being torn apart by the Milky Way.

Center: the Sun. Everything is plotted from our point of view. The radius slider sets how far out you see.
Blue: galaxies at their real 3D positions. Nearby ones have measured distances (UNGC); farther ones use redshift (2MRS, ~40,000 galaxies).
Purple: dwarf galaxies and small stellar systems around the Milky Way and Andromeda.
Gold: Boötes III, the current target.
Green dots and lines: IceCube neutrinos. 275 high-energy neutrino alerts detected under the South Pole ice. We know which direction each came from, not how far, so each is drawn on the edge of the view with a line back to Earth.
◐ SKY MAP flattens the whole sky like a world map, where neutrino directions and their error circles are easy to read. Pick an overlay to lay a whole sky underneath: dark matter (Planck CMB lensing), the microwave background, gamma rays, X-rays, visible light, infrared, hydrogen gas, radio, or IceCube's own 10-year excess map.
Red: Fermi gamma-ray sources. Over 7,000 objects seen by NASA's Fermi telescope, mostly blazars (jets from giant black holes) and pulsars. Like the neutrinos, they are directions only, drawn on the edge.
Targets ranks nearby galaxies by how many IceCube neutrinos arrived from their direction, next to how many would land there by chance. Boötes III stays on top.

A neutrino or gamma ray that lines up with a galaxy is a coincidence to look into, not a detection. The cross-match panel counts angular matches; it does not compute significance.
